## Quantum Physics

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/QuantumPhysics.png)

### Energy Quantization

- **Definition:** This principle states that physical systems possess only discrete energy levels. Transitions occur through the absorption or emission of fixed amounts called quanta.

- **Example:** Electrons in an atom occupy specific orbitals. Moving between them requires a photon with the exact energy difference.

- **Analogy:** This is similar to a staircase. One can stand on specific steps but never in the empty space between them.

### Wave-Particle Duality

- **Definition:** Quantum entities such as photons or electrons exhibit both wave and particle properties depending on the experimental setup.

- **Example:** In the Double-Slit Experiment electrons create an interference pattern as waves yet are detected at localized positions as particles.

- **Analogy:** This is like a cylinder that appears as a circle from one side and a rectangle from another. It is a single object revealing different faces depending on the perspective.

### Observable and Operator

- **Definition:** An Observable is a measurable physical quantity such as position or momentum. An Operator is a mathematical function that acts on a quantum state to extract the value of that observable.

- **Example:** If the quantum state is a file then the Operator is the software that reads its metadata.

- **Analogy:** This is like using a thermometer to reveal the temperature of a room.

### Eigenstate and Eigenvalue

- **Definition:** An Eigenstate is a specific state where the measurement of a particular observable yields a definite result. The Eigenvalue is that specific numerical result.

- **Example:** An electron in an energy eigenstate always yields the same energy value upon measurement.

- **Analogy:** This is like a radio preset. Selecting a specific station always provides that exact frequency.

### Probability and Statistical Interpretation

- **Definition:** Quantum mechanics uses the Born rule to predict the probability distribution of measurement outcomes rather than deterministic paths.

- **Example:** The wave function indicates the probability density of finding an electron in a particular region.

- **Analogy:** This is like a heat map showing where a player spends time on a field instead of the exact coordinates at every second.

### Schrödinger Equation

- **Definition:** This is the fundamental partial differential equation that describes how the probability amplitude or wave function of a physical system evolves over time.

- **Example:** This equation is used to determine the stationary states and allowed energy levels of an atom.

- **Analogy:** This is like a weather model that predicts how a storm system will shift and develop over time across a map.

### Superposition

- **Definition:** A quantum system can exist in a linear combination of all its possible states simultaneously until a measurement is performed.

- **Example:** A qubit represents a coherent superposition of both 0 and 1 until it is read by a detector.

- **Analogy:** This is like a spinning coin. It remains a blur of both heads and tails until the spinning stops.

### Wave Function Collapse

- **Definition:** Measurement causes a quantum system to transition from a superposition of multiple potential outcomes into a single eigenstate.

- **Example:** An electron exists in a cloud of probabilities until detection forces the system to manifest in only one localized position.

- **Analogy:** This is like a lottery draw. Before the draw every ticket is a potential winner but the act of drawing the number collapses all possibilities into a single reality.

### Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

- **Definition:** This is a fundamental limit stating that non-commuting observables such as position and momentum cannot be known with arbitrary precision simultaneously.

- **Example:** Increasing the accuracy of the position measurement necessarily increases the uncertainty in the momentum measurement.

- **Analogy:** This is like a blurred motion photo. Capturing a clear image of the object freezes the motion while capturing the motion blurs the object.

### Principle of Complementarity

- **Definition:** This principle states that quantum objects possess mutually exclusive properties that require different experimental conditions to be observed.

- **Example:** An experiment designed to determine which path a particle takes will destroy the wave interference pattern.

- **Analogy:** This is like an optical illusion where two different perspectives are needed to see the whole picture but the focus can only remain on one at a time.

### Pauli Exclusion Principle

- **Definition:** This rule states that no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state within a quantum system simultaneously.

- **Example:** This principle explains the structure of the periodic table and why electrons occupy different shells in an atom.

- **Analogy:** This is like a theater with numbered seats. Two people cannot sit in the same chair at once.

### Quantum Entanglement

- **Definition:** This is a phenomenon where particles become correlated such that the quantum state of each particle cannot be described independently of the others.

- **Example:** Measuring the spin of one entangled particle instantly determines the spin of its partner regardless of the spatial separation.

- **Analogy:** This is like magic gloves. Putting the right glove on in one city instantly makes the glove in another city the left one.

### Quantum Tunneling

- **Definition:** This is a quantum mechanical process where a particle penetrates a potential energy barrier that is higher than its kinetic energy.

- **Example:** This allows nuclear fusion in the sun by enabling protons to overcome the Coulomb barrier.

- **Analogy:** This is like a ghost walking through a wall. The particle simply appears on the other side without going over the top.

## Quantum Computing

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/QuantumComputing.png)

### Qubit

The primary unit of quantum information that utilizes quantum mechanical properties. It exists in a state representing multiple logical values simultaneously until observed.

### Quantum State

The complete description of a quantum system at a specific moment. It encompasses all possible measurement probabilities and the relationship between its constituent parts.

### Bra-Ket (Dirac Notation)

A specialized symbolic language designed to represent quantum states and their interactions. It provides a standardized way to denote state vectors and their mathematical counterparts.

### Probability Amplitude

A fundamental value associated with a quantum state that determines the likelihood of the system collapsing into a specific outcome upon interaction with a measuring device.

### Quantum Interference

The phenomenon where different computational paths overlap. This allows for the strategic reinforcement of correct solutions and the systematic cancellation of incorrect ones.

### Unitary Evolution

The continuous and reversible transformation of a quantum system over time. This process ensures that the total information within the system is preserved during computational operations.

### Quantum Gate

A logical operation that modifies the state of qubits by applying specific physical interactions. It serves as the fundamental block for constructing quantum algorithms.

### Measurement

The process of extracting information from a quantum system which causes the superposition to collapse. This forces the system into a single and definite classical state.

### No-Cloning Theorem

A fundamental principle of quantum mechanics stating that it is physically impossible to create an identical and independent replica of an arbitrary and unknown quantum state.

### Decoherence

The unwanted interaction between a quantum system and its environment that leads to the decay of quantum properties. This effectively turns quantum information into classical noise.

### Density Matrix

A generalized framework used to describe the state of a quantum system. It is particularly effective for modeling mixed states that occur when a system is influenced by noise or environmental factors.

### Quantum Speedup

The measurable advantage in efficiency regarding time or memory that a quantum algorithm achieves over the most advanced classical algorithms for a specific problem.

## Quantum Architecture

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/QuantumArchitecture.png)

### Types of Physical Qubits

The various technological implementations used to create qubits such as superconducting circuits or trapped ions. Each method offers different advantages regarding stability and control.

### Logical vs Physical Qubits

The distinction between the actual hardware components and the error resistant units they form. Multiple physical qubits are often grouped together to create one stable logical qubit.

### Quantum Processing Unit (QPU)

The hardware component that performs quantum operations on qubits. It serves as the physical engine of a quantum computer by executing logic gates and managing state transformations.

### Quantum Register

A hardware or logical structure composed of multiple qubits that work in unison. It stores and processes a high volume of information through collective quantum states.

### Quantum Circuit

A structured sequence of quantum gates and measurements organized to perform a computational task. It traces the flow of information from initialization to the final result.

### Repeated Measurement and Readout

The practice of executing a quantum circuit multiple times to collect statistical data. This is necessary because individual quantum results are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

### Quantum Error Correction

A collection of protocols and codes designed to protect quantum information from noise. It functions by distributing logical data across multiple physical qubits to detect and fix errors.

### Universal Quantum Computer

A device capable of performing any quantum computation by applying a universal set of quantum gates to qubits. It represents the theoretical gold standard for general purpose quantum computing.

### Topological Quantum Computing

A robust approach to quantum computing that uses the spatial properties of certain particles to store information. This method provides natural protection against environmental noise.

### Quantum Communication

The use of quantum states to transmit data between different locations. This enables secure information exchange through unique properties like teleportation and cryptographic key distribution.

## Quantum Gates

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/QuantumGates.png)

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/BlockSphere.jpg)

In quantum computing, quantum gates are unitary operations applied to qubits. They transform the quantum state of one or more qubits and are the fundamental building blocks of quantum circuits. A qubit state can be represented as |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ where α and β are complex amplitudes.

### Hadamard Gate (H)

- **Logical Purpose:** Create superposition from computational basis states

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, |1⟩ → (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2

- **Logical Role:** Enables quantum parallelism and interference and changes measurement basis

- **Common Uses:** Initialization of algorithms, creating superposition registers, Grover's and Shor's algorithms

### Pauli-X Gate (Bit Flip)

- **Logical Purpose:** Flip the logical value of a qubit

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → |1⟩, |1⟩ → |0⟩

- **Logical Role:** Equivalent to classical NOT and used in state initialization and conditional logic

- **Common Uses:** State preparation, reversible computation

### Pauli-Y Gate

- **Logical Purpose:** Perform bit flip combined with phase change

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → i|1⟩, |1⟩ → −i|0⟩

- **Logical Role:** Combines state inversion with phase rotation

- **Common Uses:** Quantum simulations, rotational decompositions

### Pauli-Z Gate (Phase Flip)

- **Logical Purpose:** Apply a phase flip to |1⟩ without changing probabilities

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → |0⟩, |1⟩ → −|1⟩

- **Logical Role:** Phase manipulation and interference control

- **Common Uses:** Grover's marking step (state selection in search), phase-based algorithms, error correction

### S Gate (Phase π/2)

- **Logical Purpose:** Apply a π/2 phase shift to |1⟩

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → |0⟩, |1⟩ → i|1⟩

- **Logical Role:** Precise phase control and building block for more complex gates

- **Common Uses:** Circuit decompositions, phase adjustments

### T Gate (Phase π/4)

- **Logical Purpose:** Apply a π/4 phase shift to |1⟩

- **Transformation:** |0⟩ → |0⟩, |1⟩ → e^(iπ/4)|1⟩

- **Logical Role:** Enables universal quantum computation with H and CNOT

- **Common Uses:** Universal gate sets, fault-tolerant circuits

### Rotation Gates (Rx, Ry, Rz)

- **Logical Purpose:** Rotate qubit around Bloch sphere axes

- **Transformation Examples:** Rx(θ) rotation around X-axis, Ry(θ) rotation around Y-axis, Rz(θ) rotation around Z-axis

- **Logical Role:** Continuous transformations and arbitrary state preparation

- **Common Uses:** Quantum hardware operations, variational algorithms, simulations

### CNOT Gate (Controlled-NOT)

- **Logical Purpose:** Conditional bit flip of the target qubit

- **Transformation:** control ─●─, target ─X─ |00⟩ → |00⟩, |01⟩ → |01⟩, |10⟩ → |11⟩, |11⟩ → |10⟩

- **Logical Role:** Conditional logic and entanglement generation

- **Common Uses:** Bell states, quantum algorithms, entanglement circuits

- **Universality:** Part of the {H, T, CNOT} universal gate set

### CZ Gate (Controlled-Z)

- **Logical Purpose:** Apply a phase flip if both qubits are |1⟩

- **Transformation:** |00⟩ → |00⟩, |01⟩ → |01⟩, |10⟩ → |10⟩, |11⟩ → −|11⟩

- **Logical Role:** Conditional phase and entanglement creation

- **Common Uses:** Phase-based algorithms, entanglement circuits

### Toffoli Gate (CCNOT)

- **Logical Purpose:** Flip target qubit if both control qubits are |1⟩

- **Transformation:** control1 ─●─, control2 ─●─, target ─X─

- **Logical Role:** Reversible classical AND operation in quantum circuits

- **Common Uses:** Quantum arithmetic, reversible computing, error correction

### SWAP Gate

- **Logical Purpose:** Exchange states of two qubits

- **Transformation:** |01⟩ → |10⟩, |10⟩ → |01⟩

- **Logical Role:** Quantum information routing

- **Common Uses:** Hardware connectivity, qubit movement between registers

## Grover's Algorithm

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/Grover.png)

Classical computers struggle to search through large unsorted datasets. For example, if there are 1 billion items, a classical algorithm may need to check each item one by one, which can take a very long time. The time required grows proportionally with the number of items, making this approach inefficient for massive datasets.

Grover's Algorithm, developed by Lov K. Grover in 1996, is a quantum search algorithm that offers a significant speedup. It takes advantage of quantum amplitude amplification and interference to increase the probability of finding the correct solution, without needing to check every item individually.

The search problem is typically framed using a function that can identify whether an item is a solution. The algorithm encodes all possible items into a quantum superposition, allowing them to be considered simultaneously, and then iteratively amplifies the probability of the correct items.

This amplification process relies on two key operations. The first, called the oracle, marks the correct items by applying a phase flip. The second, called the diffusion operator, adjusts the overall probabilities so that the correct items become more likely to be measured. Each iteration increases the probability of the correct items while reducing the probability of incorrect ones through constructive and destructive interference.

As a result, Grover's Algorithm requires far fewer iterations than a classical search. For instance, in a dataset with about 1 billion items, a classical computer might need to check all 1 billion, while Grover's algorithm only requires roughly 33 thousand iterations. This reduces the search time significantly, demonstrating a clear quantum advantage for unstructured search problems.

## Shor's Algorithm

![](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafaelfgx/QuantumComputing/refs/heads/main/Images/Shor.png)

Classical computers have a very hard time factoring extremely large numbers, especially those with hundreds of digits. This difficulty is the foundation of RSA encryption, which secures much of modern digital communication.

The General Number Field Sieve (GNFS) is the most efficient classical algorithm known for factoring large numbers. However, even GNFS takes an impractical amount of time for the extremely large numbers used in modern cryptography. Factoring these numbers with classical computers is considered virtually impossible.

Shor's Algorithm, developed by Peter Shor in 1994, is a quantum algorithm that can factor large numbers efficiently. It achieves an exponential speedup compared to classical methods.

The algorithm works by transforming the factoring problem into a period-finding problem. It uses a function based on the number to be factored and a randomly chosen integer, with the goal of discovering a repeating pattern, or “period,” in this function.

Once the period is found, and if it meets certain conditions, it can be used to extract the factors of the original number.

Quantum computers exploit superposition, allowing them to consider many possible inputs at the same time. Through quantum interference, the states that match the repeating pattern are amplified.

Shor's Algorithm then applies a quantum transformation called the Quantum Fourier Transform, which converts the repeating pattern into a measurable distribution that encodes information about the period.

Finally, a classical computation step interprets this measurement to determine a candidate period. If the period satisfies the necessary conditions, the algorithm calculates values that produce non-trivial factors of the original number. If not, the process is repeated with a new random starting value.
